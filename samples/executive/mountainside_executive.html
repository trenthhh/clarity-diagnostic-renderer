The Mountain That Hides Itself

A short executive review of Mountainside Dental Group's five-location digital footprint.
Prepared for · Mountainside Dental Group Prepared by · Trent Wehrhahn, H-Cube Marketing Date · May 2026

I noticed Mountainside's La Quinta office while traveling in Southern California recently, and after a quick look at the broader network the same impression held: this is a strong, established independent dental group. It looks like the kind of practice the digital presence should be reinforcing at every touchpoint. From the outside, it appears to be doing some of that work, but not as consistently as the network deserves.

Mountainside appears to have already done the hard part: clinical scale, multi-location presence, patient trust, and twenty-two years of independent ownership. The opportunity is that the digital system does not yet seem to make that strength as visible as it could be.

This document is a short executive review of the patterns I'd want to talk through, ordered by what looks like the highest leverage. It is meant as a starting frame for a conversation, not a finished plan.

Executive Summary

Five Patterns Worth Discussing

From the outside, five patterns stood out across the network. Each shows up at multiple locations, which is the reason they are worth treating as network-level — they tend to respond best to one coordinated fix rather than five separate ones.

  1. 1

    Appointment friction across the network

    All five appointment paths appear to route to callback-style forms rather than real-time booking. For a single relationship-driven office, that is reasonable. For a five-location network with paid traffic and strong local demand, it tends to introduce conversion friction at the moment of highest intent.

    Patient intent is highest the moment they try to schedule. Anything that adds wait time at that moment risks losing the booking to the next listing.

  2. 2

    The Google Business Profiles appear to operate like separate practices

    From the outside, the five GBPs appear inconsistent across naming, categories, descriptions, posting cadence, photo counts, and how each location's specific differentiators are surfaced. Each looks self-contained rather than part of a coordinated network.

    For local practices, GBP is often the highest-intent conversion layer Google offers. Coordinating it across five locations is a network-level lift.

  3. 3

    Practice history is underrepresented or inconsistent

    Some of the acquired locations appear to show different founding dates in hero copy versus body or schema content. The continuity story for those practices — and the broader Mountainside / Loma Linda story — appears underused.

    History and continuity are trust signals, especially for acquired practices. Inconsistent dates can quietly undercut what is otherwise distinctive.

  4. 4

    Homepage trust signals appear to understate network scale

    The homepage references roughly 5,000+ happy patients while other network materials reference about 25,000+ patients across the five locations. The number that should anchor first-impression credibility seems set well below the network's actual scale.

    A trust counter is one of the highest-leverage first-impression elements on a homepage. It should reinforce the scale already earned.

  5. 5

    Social and legacy digital assets are fragmented

    The social footprint appears split across multiple small location-level pages, and a legacy Dr. Wolfram domain still appears in branded search. From a patient's cross-channel research, the network is harder to read than its real scale suggests.

    Patients increasingly validate practices across multiple channels before booking. The network may appear smaller and less coordinated than it actually is.

Two Worked Examples

What This Looks Like in Practice

Two examples that make the pattern map concrete without going into the full per-location detail. Both are network-level — fixing them once produces lift across the five locations.

Example 1 · Conversion friction

High-intent patients appear to end at a callback form

Across the five locations, appointment requests appear to route through forms that note the office will contact the patient to confirm. For a single, relationship-driven office, that is a perfectly reasonable choice. For a five-location network running paid traffic, organic traffic, and strong local demand, it tends to introduce conversion friction — particularly outside business hours.

This appears to be a high-leverage test opportunity rather than a problem to solve all at once.

Suggested next step

Pilot real-time booking at La Quinta first. Configure it only for straightforward appointment types — cleanings, recall, emergency exams — and keep complex cases in the existing callback flow. Measure lift over 90 days, particularly in after-hours bookings, before rolling to the other four locations.

Example 2 · Brand integrity

The network's strongest story is not fully visible

Mountainside's independent, family-owned, no-DSO/no-private-equity story is distinctive in 2026. The Yucaipa origin, the Loma Linda dental tradition, and the continuity of the acquired practices are meaningful trust signals. From the outside, it appears that some of that history is either underused, presented inconsistently across locations, or not carried through into GBP descriptions and location-page hero copy.

Suggested next step

Clarify founding dates across the location pages, separate "practice founded" from "joined Mountainside," and distribute the network and owner story consistently across homepage, location pages, GBP descriptions, and social bios.

The brand promise of HONEST Dentistry becomes more powerful when the digital footprint is equally clear, consistent, and specific.
